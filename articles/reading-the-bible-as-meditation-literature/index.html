How to Read

Reading the Bible as Meditation Literature

6 min read

Most of us read the way we were trained to read the news: quickly, for information, once. We scan for the point, extract it, and move on. It's an efficient way to handle an email. It's a terrible way to read the Bible.

The biblical authors didn't write for skimming. They wrote what's sometimes called meditation literature — texts designed to be read slowly, spoken aloud, and returned to again and again over a lifetime.

Written to be lingered over

The very first psalm describes the ideal reader as someone who "meditates" on Scripture "day and night." The Hebrew word for meditate, hagah, doesn't mean to empty your mind. It means to mutter, to chew, to speak the words under your breath — like a person turning something over and over.

That's a clue about how these texts were meant to be encountered. Not consumed once and filed away, but inhabited.

The point isn't to finish the passage. The point is to be shaped by it.

What slows you down reveals the design

When you slow down, you start to notice what fast reading hides: repeated words, deliberate patterns, echoes of earlier scenes. The authors packed meaning into structure. A word that appears seven times, a scene that mirrors another chapters away — these are invitations to pause and connect.

Speed reads past all of it. Meditative reading lets it surface.

A simple practice

Try this: take a short passage and read it out loud, slowly. Read it again. Notice a word or image that stands out and sit with it. Ask why the author chose it, and where else it shows up. Don't rush to a conclusion.

You'll cover less ground — and understand far more. That's not a failure of the method. That's the whole point.
